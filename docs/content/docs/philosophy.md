## Practical Paranoia

You don't know the right way, you never will. Even if you do, you won't for
long. A process for finding good answers is way more valuable than good answers
themselves.

The most valuable skill a software developer can have is the magical ability
to reach deep into the unknown. Feel around, sense rabbit holes, avoid them.
Then use what they find to build something. There isn't a step by step for
this, simply abstract forces to be respected:

- **Complexity is the enemy.** Hard to understand, easy to break. Testability,
  reviewability, onboarding, modifiability, etc. are almost always thrashed by
  complexity. It's a deal with the devil you need to be extremely careful
  about committing to.
- **Dependencies should earn their keep.** Every external package is a
  *liability*. I try to only use them when they solve a non-trivial problem.
  Less is more.
- **Tackle the hard parts first.** This is usually defining what you're making
  and why. Front heavy. The hardest bit is restricting scope, committing to
  *not* do stuff. For prod release infra you can definitely do more than
  Sprout, but I wouldn't do less.
- **Write (or generate) for humans.** Even if you're shipping releases 100%
  generated by an agent, they should be organized, logical, and readable by you.
  That type of code is what LLMs were trained on and what they work best with.
  If the agent has to read an entire codebase to understand what it's looking
  at only for a small edit, accuracy will be crap, and you'll blow through
  your token budget instantly. More or less the same for humans.
- **Be careful with AI.** If not, you'll end up with a patchwork feeling
  codebase that has all the downsides of complex code with none of the upsides.
  Design docs, knowing when to batch changes, searchable code, predictable
  patterns, understanding the domain, etc. All this gets more important when
  using AI, not less. If you do use it, ask a billion questions. Pretend it's a
  professor who never gets tired. Look, you don't know what you don't know, ya
  know?
- **Discipline.** Stop chasing hyperscale fantasies. Most software should
  remain small enough for individuals to understand, own, and operate.
- **It's not a phase mom!** On your spiritual journey there will be a phase
  where you wanna make everything generic and reusable. I get it, we all go
  through it. It inflates scope and complexity, your ability to actually finish
  anything goes to 0. Yay, genericide <3 Eventually you'll find a happy medium
  that's way less than you thought it'd be.
- **Just Breathe.** Build out / explore (duplicate implementation of things is
  fine), then consolidate / abstract when it works. The duplicate stuff informs
  your abstractions, blah blah blah. Many iterations of this is what makes good
  software. Sorry friend, there aren't really any shortcuts. Btw this is biased
  towards solo dev, harder to do small nice PRs when doing this. But damn it's
  a hell of a way to brute force the unknown.

Secret last one, don't treat anything as absolute, rarely anything actually
is.

## Why a template and not a framework?

Because it's more generic.

No, but seriously. In reality even though many CLI apps / daemons share
similar needs, the amount of variance there is far too great for a
configuration or patch system. The more practical route is a working common
base. Small and understandable, fits in a person's head. They can quickly see
if it fits their use case, or where to modify it if it doesn't.

Adding more config would make the implementation overflow "headspace".
As is, Sprout is robust, simple, and understandable. Currently filling that
headspace with comfortable headroom. A Lagrange point between flexibility
and complexity.

I'm inherently a little distrustful of centralization and dependency.
Diversity, independence, etc. Unironically this stuff is important.
Scroll through npm or cargo for a while and you'll find countless dead
frameworks, all bankrupt by abstraction tax, vague scope, or devs
avoiding learning by creating.

tldr - framework only makes sense if the vast majority of users are near-identical
cases that need to be upgraded in lockstep. That obviously isn't the case here.
It's your baby, take responsibility <3

## Why not use Haskell or Rust?

No amount of category or type theory will build a dashboard for you. In
reality you'll burn / replace most of the code you write regardless of the
paradigm it's based on. To build as though you won't, to have your tools
require that.. is recreation at best. Correctness isn't everything and agility
/ speed isn't nothing. It’s hard to take the functional pitch seriously when
so many of its loudest proselytizers dismiss those tradeoffs outright. I assume
that's just a vocal minority, people engaged in intellectual masturbation,
which, look, I'm not above. I'm intellectually gooning right now.

Rust mostly dodges this, but there's still increased friction compared to Go,
Python, etc., and for most projects it isn't really worth it. Though, for big
complex stuff or people who already know it well, it's a solid choice.
